How to Brief an External Trainer: A Checklist for HR Leaders Without an L&D Team

Hiring a specialist trainer is straightforward. Briefing one well is what determines whether the session lands or wastes a day. Here is the checklist we use.

By Geecom Learning May 2026 9 min read

Last updated: May 2026

The short answer: a good external trainer can change how a team operates in a single day. The variable is rarely the trainer's calibre. It is the brief. Most disappointing sessions are traceable to a thin brief that did not name the audience, the outcome, or the constraints clearly enough. This article is the eight-item checklist we use with clients before we start sourcing.

  • U.S. companies spent an estimated $101.8 billion on training in 2022–23, with roughly 13% going to outside products and services (Training Magazine, 2023 Training Industry Report).
  • Average direct learning expenditure per employee was $1,220 in 2023 (ATD, 2024 State of the Industry).
  • Career development is the top L&D priority at most organisations, but only a minority of L&D teams say their managers actively support it (LinkedIn, 2025 Workplace Learning Report).

Why the brief matters more than the trainer’s CV

The first time an HR or operations lead at a small or mid-sized company hires an external trainer, the instinct is to focus on the trainer. Vet the credentials, watch a sample, ask for references. All sensible. None of it matters if the brief is thin.

Specialist trainers are professional readers of briefs. The good ones can deliver a strong session against a clear, narrow brief and a weak session against a vague, expansive one. The trainer’s job is not to invent your priorities. It is to translate them into a session that lands. If the brief leaves the priorities unstated, the trainer will guess. Their guess will be informed by what most of their clients want, which is rarely what you want.

The good news is that writing a usable brief takes about 45 minutes. It does not require an L&D background. It requires a willingness to be specific.

The eight things every brief should answer

This is the checklist. Walk through it in order. If you cannot answer one of the items, that is the conversation you need to have internally before you start sourcing, not after.

1. Audience: who is actually in the room

Name the role, the seniority, and the prior exposure to the topic. Twelve frontline supervisors who have managed teams for 1 to 3 years and have never had formal management training is useful. Our managers is not. Include the audience size and whether attendance is mandatory. A trainer prepares differently for 8 self-selected enthusiasts than for 40 people who were told to be there.

2. Outcome: what changes by the end of the day

Write the outcome as a sentence that starts with By the end of the session, participants will be able to…. If you cannot finish that sentence, the session is not ready to be scoped. Vague outcomes like raise awareness or build a stronger culture are signals to slow down, not accelerate. They lead to sessions that everyone enjoys and no one acts on.

3. Format: workshop, lecture, hands-on, or facilitated discussion

The same content can be delivered in four different ways and the choice changes everything: pacing, group size, room layout, materials, even who is qualified to teach it. State the format you want. If you are unsure, name the constraint that decides it for you (most of our team learns by doing, so we need hands-on, or we have one shot at this and the leadership team needs to see structure, so it should be a structured workshop).

4. Duration and timing

Specify the date window, the available hours, and whether the session must fit inside a working day. Indicate whether prework is acceptable and how much. Half a day with two hours of prework is a different proposition from a full day with no prework. Trainers price and design accordingly.

5. Existing capability and prior training

Tell the trainer what the audience already knows. The fastest way to lose a room is to spend 90 minutes on material they covered in onboarding. The fastest way to lose them on the other end is to start above their head. List any related training the audience has had in the last 2 years, and any internal documentation, policies, or playbooks the trainer should reference.

6. Constraints: regulatory, technical, cultural

Capture the things that are non-negotiable. Examples: the session must reference a specific regulatory framework, the trainer must be cleared to be on a worksite, certain language is sensitive in your context, the room cannot be reconfigured, the audience cannot use personal devices. Constraints that are obvious to you are invisible to the trainer until you write them down.

7. Budget envelope

Share a range. Withholding the budget so vendors compete is a procurement habit that does not translate to specialist trainers. The same topic can be delivered as a 90-minute keynote or a 2-day immersive programme depending on the budget. Most experienced trainers will decline to quote without a number, because the design space is too wide. A range like $5,000 to $9,000 plus expenses gets you a usable proposal in 2 to 3 days. No range gets you a polite delay.

8. Definition of qualified

This is the item most briefs skip and the one that determines who you end up with. Write down what qualified means for this specific session. Lived experience as a practitioner. Recent regulatory authority. A teaching credential. A track record of delivering in your sector. Two of the four. The trainer market is large and varied; without your definition, you will be shown candidates who are technically credentialled but not the right fit.

Our view, from working with HR leaders

“The brief is the cheapest investment you will make in the engagement. An hour spent answering these eight items will save 2 weeks of rework, a wrong-fit shortlist, and a session your audience does not remember. Trainers will ask for the missing pieces eventually. The question is whether you choose what is in the brief, or let the gaps choose for you.”

— Geecom Learning, Vancouver, BC

Three things to leave out

A good brief is short. It is also disciplined about what it does not include. Three categories of content tend to creep in and dilute the rest.

Internal politics

You do not need to explain to the trainer that the VP of Operations was sceptical and the CEO had to push this through. The trainer is not your therapist and the politics are not their problem. If a stakeholder needs to be managed, manage them separately. The brief is for the work.

Solutions disguised as requirements

If the brief specifies the slide template, the breakout structure, and the exact exercises, you have hired a delivery agent, not a trainer. Specialists deliver well when they own the design. Tell them the outcome and the constraints. Let them propose the method. If you do not trust their method, you have hired the wrong specialist, not written the wrong brief.

Padding

Skip the company history, the values statement, and the page about your culture journey. None of it changes how the session is designed. If a trainer needs context on the company, they will ask. Length in a brief is not a signal of seriousness. Specificity is.

How to test a candidate against your brief

Once the brief is in front of a shortlisted trainer, the screening conversation should take 30 to 45 minutes. Three questions reveal almost everything you need to know.

Walk me through how you would structure the day. A capable trainer can sketch a session arc on the call: opening, anchor activities, moments where the room shifts, a close. If they cannot, they are not ready to deliver against your brief.

What would you adjust if you only had half the time? The answer separates designers from script-readers. A designer can identify the parts that earn their keep and explain what gets cut. A script-reader cannot.

What is the part of this brief you find weakest? The strongest candidates push back. They say the outcome is too broad, or the audience description does not match the format, or the budget will not buy what you are asking for. Take the pushback seriously. It is the most useful free consulting you will get all year.

When to hand the sourcing to someone else

Writing a good brief is the inexpensive part. Finding the right specialist to deliver against it is the part that quietly absorbs weeks. The trainers worth hiring rarely advertise. They sit inside professional associations, regulatory bodies, research institutions, and specialist consultancies. Each one has a different way of being reached, a different way of quoting, and a different lead time.

If your brief is clear and you have time to work the channels, do it yourself. If your brief is clear and you do not, that is the point at which a managed sourcing service earns its fee. We built our specialist trainer sourcing service for exactly this scenario: you give us the brief, we come back with a vetted shortlist and a placement plan, and you skip the calendar weeks of cold outreach.

Either way, the work starts with the brief. Get the brief right and the rest is logistics.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a training brief be?

One page. A brief that runs longer than a page is usually doing two jobs at once: scoping the work and lobbying for it internally. Strip the lobbying. A trainer needs the audience, the outcome, the format, the duration, the constraints, the budget, and your definition of qualified. That fits on one page.

What is the difference between a training brief and an RFP?

A brief describes the problem. An RFP is a procurement document that asks vendors to compete on a defined scope of work. For a single workshop or short engagement, a brief is sufficient and you do not need an RFP. RFPs make sense for multi-year learning programmes, panel arrangements, or anything that has to go through formal procurement.

How do I describe the audience without being vague?

Name the role, the seniority, and the prior exposure to the topic. Twelve frontline supervisors who have managed teams for 1 to 3 years and have never had formal management training is useful. Our managers is not. The trainer needs to know whether to start at fundamentals or move quickly past them, and that decision is made from your audience description.

Should I share the budget with the trainer up front?

Yes. Withholding the budget so vendors compete is a procurement habit that does not translate to specialist trainers. Most experienced trainers will decline to quote without a number, because the same topic can be delivered as a 90-minute keynote or a 2-day immersive programme depending on what you are willing to spend. Sharing a range gets you a useful proposal faster.
